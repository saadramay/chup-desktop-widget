3a TURN 3 FIRST RUN — TEN STEPS ON 2a

Nothing to read, one thing to watch

A 216px sliver cannot explain itself from the inside, and a first-time doctor has three states they will never find on their own: the hover reveal, the action's second and third faces, and the two sizes that hold the list. So the tour does not describe the widget — it drives it. Each step puts the panel into the exact state it is talking about and rings a hairline round the one region in question, so every claim is the live thing rather than a picture of it. The card sits in the opposite corner of the screen from the widget and never covers it. Ten steps, a skip on every one, and a closing card for the three lines that appear on their own. It runs once per install; afterwards Help in the ⋯ menu, under the three sizes, starts it again from step one. The copy is short because a doctor reading it is between patients, and it speaks to them directly as anda — the institutional third person Chup's documents use is right for a policy brief and wrong for a person being shown a button. It is written in Bahasa Malaysia rather than translated into it: no sentence is an English sentence with Malay words in it, and nothing measures the widget at the doctor in pixels. Function names keep their English, because that is what is printed on the button they are being told to press. Alert, End Visit, No-Show, Help, Sign out, the three sizes, NOW and the three self-appearing lines are quoted verbatim rather than translated, and the industry's English stays English too — queue, token, grip, reveal, undo, WhatsApp — exactly as Chup's own Malay documents keep behavior engine and auto-retiming. TURN 2 A SMALLER FLOOR — 1a BECOMES MEDIUM

Next at rest, now on hover

The patient in the room is in the room — the consultant is looking at them. What the overlay is for is the person on the other side of the door, so at rest the sliver shows next: shortened name, position in the session, appointment time and how long they have already been waiting. Moving the cursor onto the panel reveals now above it, at roughly double the resting size — the patient being seen under their full name, their position and how long the consultation has run. Moving it onto the action instead does not resize the panel: the button cannot move out from under the cursor, and the two lines swap in place to name the person that press will affect, so Alert reads out the patient it is about to notify before it is sent. The wait meter is the widget's bottom edge at all three sizes, costing 2px rather than a row — and that whole bottom band, next line and action and meter, holds still while Medium and Large grow the queue upward above it. Size runs 216 × 40 / 128 / 208: one width, three depths, against today's 284 × 208 / 316 / 476.

The resting panel is about the door, not the room: who is next, their position in the session, when they were due and how long they have already waited — the four facts that decide whether to press Alert. The bottom-edge meter tracks that same person, so a mostly red edge means the next patient is far past the twenty-minute limit. The consultation in progress moves to the reveal, at roughly double the resting type: NOW, the patient's full name unabbreviated on as many lines as it needs, then the token, their position and how long they have been in the room, with its own 2px meter beneath. The room gets a meter because the door has one, but not the same threshold: twenty minutes waiting is a failure, twenty minutes in consultation is often the job. The serving meter runs against the default consultation length Chup already holds per clinic per doctor, so a room that has overrun reads red without anyone having to remember that doctor's number. Only the resting line shortens names, because it has 90px for one: Rahman Sitompul becomes Rahman S. The action target is 82 × 36 and never moves — the panel only grows when the cursor is somewhere else. Medium and Large change one thing and inherit everything else: the width never moves off 216, and the queue extends upward as 20px rows of queue token, full name and minutes waited, red past twenty. Twenty is one flat figure for the whole queue rather than a per-patient estimate — it is the threshold the backend already wires, and a single number the whole clinic shares is one a doctor can hold in their head. Upward matters. The bottom 38px — next patient, action, meter — is identical paper at all three sizes and sits the same distance from the screen corner, so Alert is in the same place whether the panel is 40px or 208px tall, and growing the panel never walks the target away from the cursor. Everything a size buys is added at the far end, in the same direction the hover reveal opens. The rows are the patient list, so they identify rather than summarise: the appointment time, the token because that is what is called out and what reaches the patient's phone, the name in full at 109px, and the minutes already waited. Four facts per 20px row, ordered for a list rather than for a single line: the time is the spine, so it leads, and the name gives up the left edge it holds at rest. What the row shares with the resting line is the pair of numbers, not the arrangement — time against minutes waited, at opposite ends of the row. The list starts where the queue starts: the top row is the next patient in line, the same person the action band below is about, set in ink while everyone behind them steps back to 72%. Nothing is skipped, so the row order is the calling order. Two numbers per row is the point of the list: the time a patient was promised against the minutes they have actually waited, red once past their expected wait — so a column of red is a session that has slipped, and the gap between the two columns is how far. The rows drop the meridiem the resting line spells out in full: every patient in one session shares it, so 3.45 PM is established once at the bottom and the list can spend those 13px on names instead. Four rows at Medium, eight at Large — a session of ten seen whole; when it has drained past that, the last slot says so rather than sitting blank. That marker is what replaces See all queue: there is nowhere further to go. The window height is fixed per size, so the panel never resizes under the cursor — and that holds when there is nothing to show: an empty queue, a failed poll or a first read keeps Large at 208 and puts its one line in the same bottom 38px the action occupies, with empty paper above. Those three states used to be a dead end. They carried no title bar, so a doctor whose queue had drained could not move the widget, resize it, reach Help or close it. The reveal now belongs to the panel rather than to the patient: hover in any state and the controls are there, under CHUP instead of NOW when there is no consultation to name. The ⋯ menu is the one thing allowed to change direction: it needs 136px of headroom and the drag clamp only reserves the reveal's, so near the top of the screen it opens downward instead of clipping. It can do that because it is chrome — flipping the action band would move the target, which is the one thing that must never happen. Alert is the one place with motion: the word becomes a bell under the cursor, and pressing it rings the bell once with a single expanding ring before the button turns over, so the notification is confirmed as sent without a toast or a line of copy. What it turns over to is End Visit, not No-Show — because the common case after a call is that the patient walks in, and the resting line picks up CALLED 1M in green in place of the raw wait, since once someone has been called the useful number is how long since the call. It counts up, and at five minutes — the grace a called patient gets — it turns red. The bottom edge follows it: while a patient is called the meter stops tracking their wait and tracks the grace window instead, because that is now the thing being waited on, filling green to five minutes and red past it. Nothing pops up and nothing demands a decision; the panel just goes from green to red at the edge and in the line, and a doctor glancing at the corner knows the patient probably is not coming. That is what No-Show is for, and it is one hover away. (In the mock one minute is two seconds, so every duration in the panel is watchable: the wait against the twenty-minute limit, the minutes since a call, the time served, and how stale the last poll is. All four run off one clock — appointment times are the only fixed numbers, because an appointment is a promise, not a measurement. The seeded waits drift four minutes and then hold: this is a moment in a session, not a simulation of an afternoon, and left to climb they would eventually turn every row red and leave the panel with nothing to signal with. Time served is capped the same way and for the same reason — it opens inside the clinic default, crosses it about eight seconds in, and holds a minute over; uncapped it read as a three-hour consultation and the green half of the serving meter was never reachable at all. Press End Visit to watch a fresh room climb from zero. Only staleness and the minutes since a call run uncapped, because both reset on their own.) That leaves No-Show as what it actually is: the exception. It lives in the reveal, under the token, a hairline button in red that only exists while a patient is called and carries their name so there is no doubt who is being marked absent. Hold it — 0.55s, the fill sweeping red — because there is no undo. The old widget had this backwards: Alert led only to No-Show, so the sole way to move the queue on was to mark somebody absent, and End Visit was unreachable. Now the button at rest is always the outcome you expect, and the outcome you dread costs a hover and a hold. Moving it: the grip at the head of the reveal is the handle — the only place the cursor turns, so nothing else on the panel pretends to be draggable. Press it and the panel follows the cursor, clamped to the screen so it cannot be dragged off, and clamped with the reveal's height reserved so it can never park somewhere now and the window controls would open past the top of the screen. It sits first in the cluster, next to the button that hands the queue over to the full Chup web app when the sliver is not enough. Try it in the stand-in screen above. Flagging it: Chup has no icon set, so the bell, the grip and the open-in-Chup arrow are additions drawn from primitives, not matched to a public icon library.

What changed, and why
NO TITLE BAR

Minimise, close and the ⋯ menu fade in on hover; the whole surface drags. Neither minimise nor close ever takes the widget off the screen: minimised it is a 44px chip holding the one number the resting line leads with, closed it is a 5px tab on the screen edge. An always-on-top tool has no tray to hide in, and a doctor who dismissed it mid-session would lose the queue. Recovers 28px of height and the logo's horizontal claim without losing a control.

SIGN IN ONCE

One tick, on by default, and a footer line that states what the next launch will actually do rather than repeating the button. Stay signed in rather than Remember me, because remembering an email and never asking for a password again are different promises and only one of them is being made. A clinic tool that asks for a password at the start of every session gets left closed, so the tick is the design: sign in on the day the widget is installed and never again. Sign out lives in the ⋯ menu for the shared-workstation case, which is the honest trade — the risk moves to a control the doctor chooses, instead of a prompt they endure daily.

STARTS ITSELF

A queue tool that has to be launched is a queue tool that is not running when the session starts — so startup is not a question the card asks. On first successful sign-in the installer registers Chup in the Windows startup list; the computer boots and the widget is already in the corner with the queue read. Nothing to tick, nothing to opt into, and one fewer decision on a card whose whole job is to be passed through once. Windows' own Startup Apps list is where it gets turned off, which is where a doctor's IT would look for it anyway. The sign-in card carries the same minimise and close as the panel, and they behave the same — a 44px chip, a 5px edge tab, both one click from back.

ONE BOX, TWO JOBS

Signing in does not close a window and open another one. The card is the widget: 1.15 seconds on an eased curve, travelling to the corner it will live in and shrinking to the size it will be, sign-in fading out in the first quarter and the resting line arriving in the last third. It is driven by the Web Animations API rather than CSS, which is not a detail: the panel re-renders every second against the clock, and both a transition and a template-declared keyframe animation get rewritten out from under the compositor mid-flight — the first is cancelled, the second restarts from frame zero, and either way the box sits still and then jumps. An animation handed to the element belongs to the element, and finishes regardless of what renders around it. The landing blink is started the same way, from the render callback, so it cannot fire before the panel it decorates exists. Nothing appears and nothing disappears, so a doctor who has just typed a password never has to go looking for what it bought them — their eye is already on the box when it becomes the thing they will use all afternoon. It lands at exactly 216 × 40, twelve from each edge, and the tour opens on step one against the panel it just became. Press Sign in in the review row under 2a to watch it.

LANDING

On contact the panel blinks three times and holds a green halo that decays over the next second and a half — long enough to say this corner, this thing, from now on, short enough that it is gone before the doctor's first patient matters. It fires once, on first run only. A widget that pulsed at the edge of vision during a consultation would be the exact failure the whole design is arguing against.

SOUND: DO NOT DOUBLE IT

The widget should not play the Alert or the long-wait sound while the Chup web app is open. Both surfaces watch the same queue, so both would fire on the same event a few hundred milliseconds apart — the doctor hears a stutter and learns to distrust it, and a doubled alert is worse than none. The rule that resolves it: whichever surface is present owns the sound, and only one is ever present. When the web app is open it keeps both sounds and the widget stays silent; when it is closed — which is most of the afternoon, and exactly why this widget exists — the widget takes them over unchanged. Nothing new to learn, and no clinic ever hears the same event twice. Note also that the widget can go further than the web app can: it is always visible, so waited too long is already being said in colour at the bottom edge. Sound is for what cannot be seen, which is why Alert — a message leaving for a patient's phone — deserves one and a slow queue mostly does not.

FIRST-RUN SOUND

Three synthesised notes, no audio files: two falling as the card travels and shrinks, one brighter tick on contact, so the ear hears the same arc the eye does. It cannot clash with anything, because it plays once in the life of the install and only ever after the doctor pressed Sign in. Sound on in the review row silences it.

WAIT METER

A 3px hairline: green up to twenty minutes, red for the overrun, with the unrounded figures beneath. Recognisable without reading — mostly red means this patient has been waiting far too long.

SERVING BAND GONE

The green bar spent 26px saying what position already says. The token is now the largest element on the surface, which is the same claim made with type instead of fill.

HOLD TO CONFIRM

No-Show writes to production and marks a real person absent. A half-second hold with a fill sweep costs no pixels and no extra step for the other two actions, which stay single-click.

SILENT UNTIL WRONG

No dot, no timestamp, no refresh. Polling only becomes visible when it fails — the offline state carries the time the queue was last read, so a stale number is never mistaken for a live one. First fetch reads Reading the queue, never No one in the queue.

NO ICONS

The eye toggle becomes the word SHOW; the avatar circle is dropped; the tick in the size menu becomes an inverted green row. Chup has no icon set and a 152px window is the wrong place to invent one.

Open questions: See all queue is dropped from all three — if it should open the web queue rather than resize, it needs a home. Undo for No-Show is not drawn; the hold is the guard instead. And 1b and 1c go under the 260 × 180 clamp, which has to be lowered in the window manager first.
